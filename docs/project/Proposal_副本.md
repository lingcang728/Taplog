# Project Proposal — Group 17

**Course:** BTH003 Usability and Interaction Design
**Project type:** Design project
**Group 17:** Huang Yitao (Product Owner), Li Anyi (Scrum Master), Huang Yixiang, Li Qikai, Lin Weicheng
**Date:** 2026-11-23

## Title

**Traceable Visual Notes for Long Talks: Helping Readers Recover Context Scattered Across a Recording**

## 1. Problem

AI note-taking tools such as Feishu Minutes, DingTalk AI Notes and Tongyi Tingwu can turn a long recording into structured notes. Many of them already let the reader click a key point and jump back to the original audio. This "one point, one timestamp" design assumes that a speaker says everything about a point in one place.

Long talks often do not work this way. A speaker may state a conclusion, and only several minutes later add the condition under which it holds, explain why, or correct it during the Q&A. A reader who jumps back to the first timestamp hears a complete, confident sentence in the speaker's own voice and believes the point has been verified. In fact the reader has heard only half of it.

We think this problem matters for two reasons. First, hearing the original voice can make readers *more* confident, not less. Second, a clean visual summary makes it even harder to notice that something is missing.

## 2. Target Users and Context

- **Users:** developers and graduate students who learn from recorded technical talks and research talks, and who later go back to check a specific claim, for example before applying the speaker's advice in their own work.
- **Context:** a single person revisiting one recorded talk of about 30–50 minutes on a laptop.
- **Out of scope:** meetings, live recording, multi-speaker diarisation, collaboration and export.

## 3. Proposed System

We will design and prototype a web application that presents a talk as a vertically scrolling illustrated note. Its key feature is a **vertical talk timeline** placed next to the page scrollbar. When the reader focuses on a key point, every moment in the recording that relates to that point is highlighted on the timeline. Each moment is labelled with its relation to the point: *source*, *added condition*, *correction*, *reason* or *example*. The reader can play any of these short clips and then return to the same reading position.

Two other layouts will be explored as alternatives before we choose a final design: an inline hint bar under each point, and a diagram with a side panel.

The interface and notes will be in English. All talks we use are Chinese-language technical talks, so that our Chinese-speaking participants are not limited by listening comprehension. The user test uses two talks with clear long-distance caveats and one control talk without them. The notes and clip links will be generated by an AI model from real subtitles and then checked by the team (human-in-the-loop). We will report clearly which parts are AI-generated and which are hand-made.

## 4. Design Principles

The design will follow at least the following principles from the course:

1. **Visibility of system status** (Nielsen): show which clips have been played and which have not.
2. **Error prevention** (Nielsen): after the reader plays only the first clip, warn that the point is qualified later in the talk.
3. **Recognition rather than recall** (Nielsen): show "3 related moments, condition added at 37:50" next to each point, so that readers do not need to remember where else it was discussed.
4. **User control and freedom** (Nielsen): bounded playback, extending a clip, and one-click return to the reading position.
5. **Aesthetic and minimalist design** (Nielsen): show only the relations that change the meaning (conditions, corrections) by default, and fold the others.
6. **Gulf of evaluation and mental models** (Norman): challenge the false belief that "I heard the original audio, so I have checked it".

## 5. Method and Evaluation Plan

1. **Research:** collect about 10 real public Chinese technical talks (conference talks and research talks) with timed subtitles and measure how often a point is qualified or corrected five or more minutes later; walk through existing AI note tools with the same talk; analyse online user reviews of these tools; run short online interviews with 3–5 people who regularly watch recorded talks.
2. **Design:** generate several design alternatives, screen them against our principles, and run quick tests with three participants before choosing one.
3. **Prototype:** build one complete task flow with real talk audio.
4. **Evaluation:**
   - a cognitive walkthrough and a keystroke-level time estimate;
   - a heuristic evaluation by 3–5 evaluators from another project group;
   - a user test with 6–8 participants comparing single-timestamp notes with our design.
   
   Measures: misreading rate, task time, number of clips played, confidence, perceived effort and SUS.

**Research questions:**
- **RQ1:** Do single-timestamp notes lead readers to misunderstand points that are qualified later?
- **RQ2:** Does showing all related moments reduce misreading, and at what cost in time and effort?
- **RQ3:** Are the relation labels useful, or are they noise?
